1. Overview
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
mdp solves a small Markov decision process: a turn-based battle between two
"Pokemon-like" fighters, where one side chooses actions and the other side's
actions and the damage rolls are random. Battle.evaluate() builds the state
graph reachable from the initial state and runs value iteration on it until
the win probability of the initial state is known to within a tolerance
(0.192 in the benchmark); the result must be 0.89873589887 +- 1e-6.

Libraries. Standard library only: collections (namedtuple, defaultdict),
fractions.Fraction for exact probabilities, and pyperf. No C extension on the
hot path.

Data structures and algorithm.
  - A state is (our fighter, their fighter, extra); each fighter is a
    halfstate_t namedtuple (fixed data, hp, status, stat mods, stats). States
    are used as dict keys.
  - A graph node is a tuple whose first element says what kind of node it is
    (the code calls a node `statep`; section 3 and the slides say "state" for
    the same thing):
      (0, state)                choice node - our side picks Dig or Super Potion
      (1, state, action)        chance node - the opponent picks Water Gun
                                (p = 64/130) or Bubblebeam (p = 66/130) and the
                                side with priority moves first: Super Potion
                                always, otherwise the faster fighter; a speed
                                tie splits p evenly between the two orders
      (2, state, side, action)  chance node - the slower side's move resolves
      (4, True) / (4, False)    win / loss, fixed at 1.0 / 0.0
    (there is no kind 3; the numbering is the original author's). Successor
    lists are computed lazily and memoized in Battle.successors: choice nodes
    carry a list of next nodes, chance nodes a list of (next node, probability)
    pairs. The probabilities are exact Fractions, built in getCritDist and
    _applyActionSide1 and converted to float once the list is final;
    getDamages itself returns integer damage values, which getCritDist turns
    into a distribution.
  - topoSort() orders all reachable nodes with an explicit stack. There are
    4,823 of them, win and loss included (getSuccessorsList runs once per node:
    results/mdp/cprofile_base.txt).
  - Value iteration keeps two values per node, a lower bound dmin (starts at
    0) and an upper bound dmax (starts at 1), and sweeps the nodes in
    topological order updating both in place:
      choice node   v = max(v of successors)
      chance node   v = sum(p_i * v_i over successors)
    A node freezes once its bounds meet; the run stops when the initial node's
    bounds are within the tolerance.


2. Initial analysis
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Tools as for pyflate: py-spy for the flame graphs (Python-level frames,
rendered with the FlameGraph scripts), cProfile for exact call counts, perf
stat for the hardware counters, and perf record, run as root, on the software
clock. The hardware cycles event never samples in this guest; the software
clocks do (results/mdp/perf_events_probe.txt: cpu-clock 3,816 samples,
task-clock 3,614). report_pyflate.txt section 2 gives the PMI explanation and
the note on why an earlier version of the scripts reported no sampling event.
The guide's own form, perf record -F 999 -g under python3-dbg, is in
results/mdp/perf_report_base_dbg.txt (336K samples); the capture this report
reads is the benchmark worker under the release interpreter with
-e cpu-clock -F 997 -g, results/mdp/perf_top_base.txt (10K samples), where
tuplehash is 36.1% of the samples, _PyEval_EvalFrameDefault 18.0%, long_hash
6.4% and lookdict 4.8% - the sweep's nested-tuple dictionary keys, seen from
the C side. perf names the interpreter's C functions, not Python functions
(CPython 3.10 has no perf trampoline), so the flame graphs in results/mdp/
that this report reads are py-spy's, and the shares below are cProfile's.

Where the time goes (cProfile, one evaluate(), baseline). Recomputed from
results/mdp/cprofile_base.txt by scripts/cprofile_shares.py; shares are of
bench_mdp's cumulative time, 8.927 s under the profiler:
                                       self     cum       calls
  Battle.evaluate                      16.8%  100.0%           1
  <genexpr> sum(dmin[sp2]*p ...)       12.8%   12.8%   2,018,493
  <genexpr> sum(dmax[sp2]*p ...)       12.2%   12.2%   2,018,493
  Fraction._add                        10.5%   19.8%     360,472
  builtins.sum                          8.5%   33.5%     521,459
  Fraction.__new__                      6.3%    7.3%     489,681
  Battle.getSuccessors                  5.4%   42.9%     784,243
  Fraction forward()                    2.9%   24.6%     378,891
  builtins.max                          2.4%    6.1%     312,640
  getCritDist                           2.5%   23.1%       3,659

Reading it: a little over half the run is the value-iteration sweep itself
(evaluate's own body, the four generator expressions cProfile separates out -
the sum() pair on the chance-node path dominates, the max() pair on the
choice-node path costs about a seventh as much - and sum/max come to 52.7%),
and about a fifth is Fraction arithmetic while the successor graph is built.
The sweep is slow for a reason the py-spy flame graph does not show but the
code and the perf capture do: dmin, dmax and frozen are dictionaries keyed by
the state tuples, and every update does several lookups of a key that is a
tuple of namedtuples - each lookup hashes the whole nested structure, which
is what the tuplehash, long_hash and lookdict shares above measure.
getSuccessors() is called from inside the sweep for every state, twice per
node (once for dmin and once for dmax), and the generator expressions are
re-created each time. The Fraction cost comes from getCritDist being called
3,659 times with only THREE distinct argument tuples, recomputing the same
exact distributions over and over.

Hardware counters in the VM (results/mdp/perfstat_base.txt, perf stat -r 3
over four loops of the worker; the before/after table is in 4.1): the baseline
retires 118,538,058,891 instructions in 48,372,566,976 cycles, an IPC of 2.45,
with 24,945,970,884 branches of which 0.52% mispredict. The IPC is healthy and
the branches predict well, so this is not a stalled or badly predicted
workload - it is simply executing far more instructions than the problem
needs, which is what section 3 removes.


3. Optimizations
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Both changes are in benchmarks/mdp/run_benchmark_opt.py; the diff against the
shipped file is small and local.

    Reading order (line numbers are opt / orig; "-" means the original has no
    counterpart). The complete change is
      diff -u benchmarks/mdp/run_benchmark.py benchmarks/mdp/run_benchmark_opt.py
    and it lands in two functions:
      Battle.evaluate   250-288 / -        3.1, number the states and build the
                                           flat successor rows, once
                        290-315 / 223-243  3.1, the sweep: the same loop over
                                           those rows; dmin[sp] has become
                                           vmin[i] and sum() a running total
                        317-322 / -        3.1, write the values back into
                                           self.min / self.max / self.frozen
      getCritDist        52-74  /  44-54   3.2, the cache dict and the memoized
                                           function
    The comment at lines 243-249 of Battle.evaluate is the CPython 3.12 caveat
    discussed below in this section; the file's docstring (line 7) points to it.

3.1 Value iteration on integer-indexed flat lists
    After topoSort, evaluate() assigns each state a small integer once and
    builds, per state, a tuple of successor indices and (for chance nodes) a
    tuple of probabilities. The sweep then runs on plain lists: vmin[i],
    vmax[i], fz[i], with the successor indices already resolved. The sweep
    order, the in-place (Gauss-Seidel) update, the freezing rule and the
    order of the floating-point additions are exactly those of the original
    (sum() starts from 0 and adds left to right; so does our loop), so the
    converged values are the same doubles. At the end the values are written
    back into self.min / self.max / self.frozen so the object looks as it
    did before.

3.2 Memoized damage distributions
    getCritDist keeps a module-level dictionary from its argument tuple to the
    finished distribution; over a whole evaluation there are only three
    distinct tuples behind those 3,659 calls. The arithmetic is untouched and
    still exact, it is simply not repeated. The cached object is a plain dict
    rather than the defaultdict the function builds, so a later caller reading
    a missing key cannot silently extend it. Clearing the cache between
    evaluations changes neither the result nor the timing measurably (the
    saving is within one evaluation, not across benchmark iterations).

What we did not do: replacing Fraction by float in the probability math would
be faster still, but it changes the probabilities at the last bits and we
preferred a version whose result is provably identical to the original
rather than one that merely stays inside the tolerance.

One caveat on that identity. The original accumulates each successor sum with
sum(); our sweep keeps a running total in a plain loop, which is part of the
speedup. CPython 3.12 changed sum() to compensated (Neumaier) summation for
floats, so on 3.12 and later it is the ORIGINAL's value that moves, by about
one ulp (1.1e-16 at tolerance 0.192, against the benchmark's own 1e-6 gate).
The course VM runs CPython 3.10.12, where sum() is a plain loop and the two are
bit-identical, and every number in this report comes from that VM.
Accumulating with sum() as the original does would restore bit-equality on
every interpreter at a cost in speed; we kept the loop, since on the VM's
interpreter the two are already identical.
scripts/local_check.py compares the two versions against each other, not only
against the expected constant, and says which of the two cases it is in.

Correctness. scripts/local_check.py runs both versions:
    baseline  0.8987358988699915
    optimized 0.8987358988699915        difference 0.0
    |optimized - expected| = 8.5e-15  (gate is 1e-6)

3.3 Which of the two earned the speedup
    One ratio for two changes says nothing about which of them mattered, which
    is the same question section 3.6 of report_pyflate.txt exists to answer.
    scripts/ablation_mdp.py reverts one change at a time and times the result;
    every variant is checked against the benchmark's own tolerance before it is
    timed, so a variant that computed the wrong value could not appear.
    Measured in the VM (results/mdp/ablation.txt; the minimum of five
    repetitions per variant, the file also lists the medians). These rows are
    timed in one process to compare with each other; the 3.83x here is not a
    second headline figure, and differs from section 4.1's 3.90x because that
    one is pyperf's mean over thirty values in its own worker processes:

        baseline                                     5,007.1 ms   1.00x
        optimized (both 3.1 and 3.2)                 1,307.9 ms   3.83x
        optimized, 3.1 reverted to the dict sweep    4,158.7 ms   1.20x
        optimized, 3.2 reverted to recomputing       2,141.6 ms   2.34x

    Leave one out and the run costs 2,850.8 ms more without the flat-list
    sweep and 833.7 ms more without the memoized distributions. As in pyflate
    these are leave-one-out differences, not a decomposition - they do not
    multiply out to 3.83x - but here they are far apart enough to order
    confidently: the largest run-to-run spread across the variants is
    126.7 ms, and the gap between the two changes is 2,017.1 ms.

    The shape is the opposite of pyflate's. There the change we called the
    algorithmic one turned out to be the smallest of the three that could be
    separated; here it is almost the whole speedup. The reason is visible in
    section 2: mdp's cost was never the arithmetic, it was reaching the
    numbers - dictionary lookups on nested namedtuple keys, millions of times
    - and replacing the lookup is the only change that removes that work.
    Memoizing getCritDist removes real work too, but it is bounded by how
    often the same arguments recur, and the profile put that at 3,659 calls
    with three distinct argument tuples.

    Both variants revert by substituting source, so each one is a whole module
    built the same way. For 3.1 a function patch would in fact have worked:
    the sweep reaches getCritDist through self.getSuccessors, which resolves on
    the instance's class, not through the module globals a patched-in function
    would carry - checked by patching it and finding the memo still populated.
    That is the opposite of pyflate's move-to-front, where the optimized decode
    loop inlines the work and no patch can reach it.

4.3 Reproducibility
    Three independent runs in the same guest, each made by extracting the tree
    into an empty directory and running the scripts as a grader would. The
    shipped set is the latest; the others are results/reproducibility/ and
    results/reproducibility/run3/.

    The two earlier runs predate the 15 September revision - see section 4.3 of
    report_pyflate.txt for what changed and why the wall clocks are still
    comparable. The one change that touches this benchmark's measured path:
    the successor rows are now built with a generator expression where those
    runs appended to a list. The result is bit-identical either way, and the
    difference is part of the spread below.

        run          speedup   optimized
        shipped      3.895x    1.309 s
        second       3.820x    1.305 s
        third        3.822x    1.308 s

    The ratio holds to 2.0% across the three; the drift is in the baseline
    (5.098 s here against 4.986 s and 5.000 s, just over one standard
    deviation of pyperf's +- 0.085 s), the optimized time moves by 0.3%.
    (pyflate is less stable - 3.0% across the same three runs;
    report_pyflate.txt section 4.3 says so.) Compare any two with
    scripts/compare_runs.py.

    A fourth run, made while the shared host was busy, is excluded: it put mdp
    at 2.33 s against 1.30 s while executing the same 31.5 billion instructions,
    because "CPUs utilized" had fallen from 1.00 to 0.58 - the wall clock moved
    and the instruction count did not. script_mdp.sh records that figure for
    every run in results/mdp/contention.txt and warns below 0.95, so the reader
    can see the guest had the core rather than take it on trust.


5. Hardware acceleration proposal
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Not applicable to this benchmark: the project's single accelerator targets
pyflate (report_pyflate.txt, hw/), following the course staff's instruction
to design one hardware component for one of the two benchmarks.

For completeness, what the profile says would be the candidate here: the
value-iteration sweep is, per chance node, a sparse dot product
sum_j p_ij * V_j followed by a compare, and per choice node a max over a few
values - a sparse matrix-vector product repeated until convergence. That maps
onto the dataflow / MAC-array pattern from lecture 5 (stream the (index,
probability) pairs of each row through a multiply-accumulate unit, read V
from a small local memory, write V back), and the graph here is small enough
(a few thousand states, a few tens of thousands of edges) to sit entirely in
on-chip memory. We did not design it: after the software fix the whole run
is 1.3 s and the sweep is at most about 35% of it - that is evaluate's own
share in the cProfile table of section 4.2, and after the 3.1 rewrite it
covers the index build and the write-back in the same function as well as the
sweep, so the sweep alone is less. The exact-Fraction graph construction that
remains is not a datapath problem.


6. Conclusion
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The benchmark's cost was not the value iteration as an algorithm but how it was
indexed and accumulated: dictionary lookups keyed by nested tuples inside the
hottest loop, plus exact-arithmetic distributions recomputed thousands of times.
Indexing the graph once and memoizing the distributions gives 3.90x in the course
VM (5.098 s to 1.309 s, 74.3% less time). On the VM's CPython 3.10 the result is
the same IEEE-754 double as the original, bit for bit; on CPython 3.12 and later
it is the original that moves, by about one ulp, because sum() switched to
compensated summation there - section 3 explains the trade and why we kept the
faster form. With that qualifier stated, it is the kind of change we would be
comfortable offering to the benchmark suite itself.
